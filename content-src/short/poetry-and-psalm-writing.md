# Learning from the Psalms | How biblical poetry works.
## Parallelism: thought rhyme | Psalm 19:1-2; Psalm 103:1-2; Proverbs 15:1
Hebrew poetry rhymes **ideas** more than sounds. This is called **parallelism**: a second line answers the first.

- **Synonymous** (the second line echoes the first): "The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork" (Psalm 19:1).
- **Antithetic** (the second line contrasts): "A soft answer turneth away wrath: but grievous words stir up anger" (Proverbs 15:1).
- **Synthetic** or building (the second line extends): "Bless the LORD, O my soul, and forget not all his benefits" (Psalm 103:2).

Because it rhymes thought, biblical poetry survives translation into every language. That is a gift to poets: you can write in English, Yoruba, Igbo or Pidgin and still use this ancient form.

**Exercise**: write three couplets about God's faithfulness, one of each type.
## Images and honesty | Psalm 42:1-3; Psalm 23:1-4; Psalm 13:1-2
The psalmists think in pictures. "As the hart panteth after the water brooks, so panteth my soul after thee, O God" (Psalm 42:1). Thirst for God becomes a deer at a stream. "The LORD is my shepherd" turns care into a picture of grass, water, a rod and a valley.

Images make the invisible visible. Compare:

- Abstract: "I felt far from God."
- Image: "My prayers fell like stones into a dry well."

The Psalms are also **honest**. "How long wilt thou forget me, O LORD? for ever? how long wilt thou hide thy face from me?" (Psalm 13:1). They bring fear, anger, grief and doubt to God, and they usually move toward trust by the end (Psalm 13:5-6).

Christian poetry does not need to be cheerful all the time. It needs to be **true**: true to God and true to life.

**Exercise**: list ten images from your daily world (a danfo, a generator, a harmattan morning, a market at dusk) and write one line connecting each to something about God.

# The poet's tools | Sound, line and form.
## Sound and rhythm | Psalm 150:3-6; Ecclesiastes 12:10
Poems are made to be **heard**. Read every draft aloud.

Sound tools:

- **Rhyme**: matching end sounds (*grace / place*). Use it if it serves the poem; never twist meaning to force it.
- **Alliteration**: repeated first sounds ("the still, small sound").
- **Assonance**: repeated vowel sounds ("the cold stone of the road").
- **Rhythm and metre**: patterns of stressed and unstressed syllables. Many hymns use a steady beat, such as Common Metre (8.6.8.6 syllables), which is why many hymn texts can be sung to several tunes.
- **Repetition and refrain**: Psalm 136 repeats "for his mercy endureth for ever" in every verse.

The Preacher "sought to find out acceptable words" (Ecclesiastes 12:10). Choose words for their sound as well as their sense.
## Lines and forms | Psalm 119:1-8; Lamentations 3:22-23
Where a line ends is a decision. A **line break** creates a small pause and emphasis. Ending a line on a strong word ("fire", "cross", "home") gives it weight.

Forms to try:

- **Acrostic**: each line or stanza begins with the next letter. Psalm 119 does this with the Hebrew alphabet; Lamentations 1 to 4 also use it. Try your name or a word like GRACE.
- **Quatrain**: four-line stanzas, often rhymed ABAB or ABCB. Common in hymns.
- **Haiku-like short form**: three short lines capturing one moment (originally 5-7-5 syllables in Japanese).
- **Free verse**: no fixed rhyme or metre, but careful rhythm, image and line breaks.
- **Praise poem (oríkì style)**: many Nigerian cultures praise a person or God by heaping up names, deeds and titles. Psalm 145 does something similar.

Form is not a cage. It is a trellis that helps the poem grow.

# Writing and sharing your poems | Praise, lament and a small collection.
## Psalms of praise and lament | Psalm 100:1-5; Psalm 88:1-3; Psalm 30:11-12
Two of the most common kinds of psalm:

**Praise** (e.g. Psalm 100): a **call** to praise ("Make a joyful noise"), **reasons** ("For the LORD is good"), and often a renewed call.

**Lament** (e.g. Psalm 13, Psalm 88): an **address** to God, a **complaint** (the pain), a **request** (help), often a **statement of trust**, and a **vow of praise**. Psalm 88 is unusual: it ends in darkness, reminding us that God receives even prayers that don't resolve.

**Thanksgiving** (e.g. Psalm 30): tells the story of trouble and rescue: "Thou hast turned for me my mourning into dancing" (verse 11).

**Exercise**: write a psalm of lament about something you or your community has suffered, following the five movements. Then write a psalm of praise or thanksgiving with specific reasons, not general ones.
## Revising and sharing | Proverbs 27:17; Psalm 45:1
"My heart is inditing a good matter... my tongue is the pen of a ready writer" (Psalm 45:1).

Revising a poem:

- **Cut** extra words, especially adjectives and "very". Poems are concentrated.
- **Replace abstractions** (*love, joy, pain*) with images.
- **Check line breaks**: does each line end where it should?
- **Read aloud** for rhythm and sound.
- **Check theology**: is what you say about God true to Scripture?
- **Title**: give the reader a door into the poem.

Sharing:

- Read at a fellowship, open mic or STRONGS Writers meeting.
- Submit to church magazines, anthologies and poetry competitions (read the rules).
- Post on a blog or social media with a clear layout.
- Pair a poem with a photograph or calligraphy.

Keep originals dated. Credit any line you borrow, and never present someone else's poem as yours.
