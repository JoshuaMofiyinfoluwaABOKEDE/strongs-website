# Grammar and punctuation | The rules that cause most mistakes.
## Grammar that trips writers | Proverbs 25:11
Good grammar is not snobbery; it removes obstacles between the reader and the meaning. "A word fitly spoken is like apples of gold" (Proverbs 25:11).

Common problems:

- **Subject-verb agreement**: "The list of names **is** long" (the subject is *list*, not *names*). "Each of the students **has** a Bible."
- **Pronoun clarity**: "When Tunde met Ade, he was happy." Who was happy? Rewrite: "Tunde was happy to meet Ade."
- **Tense consistency**: don't drift between past and present in one story.
- **Confused words**:
  - *its* (belonging to it) / *it's* (it is)
  - *their* / *there* / *they're*
  - *your* / *you're*
  - *affect* (verb) / *effect* (usually noun)
  - *loose* / *lose*, *advice* (noun) / *advise* (verb)
- **Nigerian English vs international usage**: decide which your readers expect. For international readers, avoid local constructions like "on the light" (switch on the light) or "borrow me" (lend me), unless writing dialogue.
## Punctuation that matters | Nehemiah 8:8
Ezra's readers "gave the sense" (Nehemiah 8:8). Punctuation helps readers find the sense.

- **Full stop**: end sentences. Short sentences are your friend.
- **Comma**: separate items in a list, set off introductory phrases ("After the meeting, we prayed."), and surround extra information ("Paul, who wrote Romans, was a tentmaker."). Do not join two full sentences with only a comma: "I came, I left early" is a **comma splice**; use a full stop or "and".
- **Apostrophe**: possession (the church's choir, the churches' choirs) and contractions (don't). Never for plurals: *CDs*, not *CD's*.
- **Colon**: introduces a list, explanation or quotation.
- **Semicolon**: joins two closely related full sentences.
- **Quotation marks**: for exact words. Be consistent with single or double marks.
- **Capitals**: names, places, days, and pronouns for God if your publication's style uses them, consistently.
- **Exclamation marks**: rarely. One is enough!

# Line editing | Making sentences clear, smooth and consistent.
## Editing for clarity | Ecclesiastes 12:10; 1 Corinthians 14:9
"Except ye utter by the tongue words easy to be understood, how shall it be known what is spoken?" (1 Corinthians 14:9). **Line editing** improves sentences and paragraphs so readers understand them easily.

Line-editing moves:

- **Cut wordiness**: "due to the fact that" → "because"; "at this point in time" → "now"; "in order to" → "to".
- **Prefer active voice**: "Mistakes were made" → "We made mistakes."
- **Replace weak verbs with strong ones**: "make a decision" → "decide"; "give encouragement" → "encourage".
- **Break long sentences** with more than one main idea.
- **Remove repetition** of words and ideas, unless repetition is deliberate for emphasis.
- **Replace vague words** (*thing, stuff, aspect, various*) with specific ones.
- **Keep the author's voice.** Edit to clarify, not to make the writing sound like you.
## Consistency and style sheets | 1 Corinthians 14:40
"Let all things be done decently and in order" (1 Corinthians 14:40). Readers notice inconsistency even when they can't name it.

Check consistency in:

- **Spelling**: British (colour, organise) or American (color, organize). STRONGS uses British spelling.
- **Names**: the same spelling and title each time (Pastor Ade Ojo, then Pastor Ojo).
- **Numbers**: commonly words for one to nine, figures for 10 and above; figures for dates, money and percentages.
- **Bible references**: one format (John 3:16; 1 Corinthians 13:4-7) and the same version named.
- **Capitalisation and hyphenation**: decide once (e.g. *e-mail* or *email*) and keep it.
- **Headings and lists**: same style throughout.

Keep a **style sheet**: a one-page list of decisions for the document. It saves time and helps several editors work together.

# Proofreading | The final check before publication.
## A proofreading method | Luke 1:3
Luke "had perfect understanding of all things from the very first" and wrote "in order" (Luke 1:3). Careful work deserves a careful final check.

**Proofreading** catches the last errors after editing is done: typos, missing words, wrong punctuation, formatting slips.

Method:

1. **Take a break** before proofreading. Fresh eyes catch more.
2. **Print it** or change the font or size; the brain sees familiar text less carefully.
3. **Read slowly, aloud**, or follow with a finger or ruler.
4. **Read in several passes**, each for one thing: spelling; punctuation; names and numbers; headings and formatting.
5. **Read backwards** sentence by sentence to catch spelling without being carried by meaning.
6. **Check the high-risk places**: titles, headings, captions, names, dates, phone numbers, Bible references, and the first and last lines.
7. **Use spellcheck**, but do not trust it: it won't catch *form* for *from* or *pubic* for *public*.
## Proofreading checklist and tools | Proverbs 27:17
"Iron sharpeneth iron" (Proverbs 27:17). Ask someone else to proofread too; others see what we miss.

A checklist for print and online:

- [ ] Title and headings spelled correctly
- [ ] Author names and titles correct
- [ ] Dates, times, venues and phone numbers checked against the source
- [ ] Bible references checked against the Bible itself
- [ ] Quotations exact, with sources credited
- [ ] No double spaces, doubled words ("the the") or missing words
- [ ] Consistent spelling, capitals and numbers (see the style sheet)
- [ ] Links work (online)
- [ ] Page numbers, captions and contents page match (print)

Useful tools: your word processor's spelling and grammar checker, the "Find" function to search for common errors, and a style guide. Tools help; careful reading decides.

On event flyers and programmes, one wrong date or phone number can undo all the work. Check them twice.
