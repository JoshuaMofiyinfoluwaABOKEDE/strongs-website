# The preacher's life | Speaking begins with walking with God.
## A called and prepared messenger | Jeremiah 1:4-10; 2 Timothy 4:1-5; Ezra 7:10
When God called Jeremiah, he protested, "Ah, Lord GOD! behold, I cannot speak: for I am a child." God answered, "Say not, I am a child... whatsoever I command thee thou shalt speak... Behold, I have put my words in thy mouth" (Jeremiah 1:6-9). Fear is common; God's word gives courage.

Paul charged Timothy: "Preach the word; be instant in season, out of season; reprove, rebuke, exhort with all longsuffering and doctrine" (2 Timothy 4:2).

The speaker's first preparation is personal. Ezra "prepared his heart to seek the law of the LORD, and to do it, and to teach" (Ezra 7:10). Notice the order: **seek, do, teach**.

Before you prepare a message, prepare yourself:

- Pray for your hearers and for yourself.
- Let the passage speak to you first.
- Deal with any known sin.
- Depend on the Holy Spirit, not on eloquence.
## Speaking with integrity | 1 Corinthians 2:1-5; 2 Corinthians 4:2; 1 Thessalonians 2:3-6
Paul came to Corinth "not with excellency of speech or of wisdom", determined "not to know any thing among you, save Jesus Christ, and him crucified", so that their faith would "not stand in the wisdom of men, but in the power of God" (1 Corinthians 2:1-5).

Integrity in speaking means:

- **Handling the Word honestly**: "not walking in craftiness, nor handling the word of God deceitfully" (2 Corinthians 4:2).
- **No manipulation**: not using emotional pressure, guilt or tricks to get responses.
- **No flattery or greed**: Paul said "neither at any time used we flattering words... nor a cloke of covetousness" (1 Thessalonians 2:5).
- **Honest stories**: no exaggerated or borrowed testimonies told as your own.
- **Crediting sources** when you use others' ideas.
- **Living what you preach.**

The world admires clever speakers. God uses faithful ones.

# From text to message | Finding what God is saying in the passage.
## Studying the passage | Nehemiah 8:8; 2 Timothy 2:15; Acts 17:11
In Nehemiah's day the teachers "read in the book in the law of God distinctly, and gave the sense, and caused them to understand the reading" (Nehemiah 8:8). That is the heart of good preaching: making the meaning of Scripture plain.

Steps:

1. **Choose the passage** (a paragraph or short section) with prayer.
2. **Read it many times** and in context.
3. **Observe**: who, what, where, when, why; repeated words; commands; contrasts.
4. **Interpret**: what did it mean to the first hearers? What kind of writing is it?
5. **Check** your understanding with cross-references and a reliable study Bible or commentary.
6. **Apply**: what does it mean for your hearers today?

Do your own study before reading others. Commentaries help you check, not replace, your work.
## The main idea | Acts 2:14-36; Acts 13:16-41
Peter's sermon at Pentecost had one clear point: "God hath made that same Jesus, whom ye have crucified, both Lord and Christ" (Acts 2:36). Paul's sermon in Antioch built to one message: "through this man is preached unto you the forgiveness of sins" (Acts 13:38).

Every good message has **one main idea**, which comes from the main idea of the passage.

Write it in **one sentence**:

- The **subject**: what is the passage about? (e.g. "worry")
- The **complement**: what does it say about it? (e.g. "is overcome by prayer and thanksgiving that brings God's peace", Philippians 4:6-7)

Then ask:

- Can I state it in under 20 words?
- Does the passage really teach this?
- Will my hearers understand it?

If you cannot say your main idea clearly, your hearers will not catch it either.

# Structure | Building a clear introduction, body and conclusion.
## The body of the message | Matthew 7:24-27; 1 Corinthians 14:40
Most messages have three parts: **introduction**, **body** and **conclusion**. Start by building the body, then write the introduction and conclusion around it.

The **body** develops the main idea in two to four movements (points). Each point:

- **States** a clear idea that supports the main idea.
- **Explains** it from the passage.
- **Illustrates** it with an example, story or image.
- **Applies** it to life.

Ways to order points:

- Following the **flow of the passage** (often best).
- **Problem → solution → response**.
- **Questions** the passage answers (What? Why? How?).

Jesus ended the Sermon on the Mount with a clear contrast: the wise builder on rock and the foolish on sand (Matthew 7:24-27). Clear structure helps people remember.
## Introductions and conclusions | Acts 17:22-31; Joshua 24:14-15
**Introductions** (2 to 3 minutes):

- **Capture attention**: a story, question, surprising fact or current event. Paul began in Athens with their altar "TO THE UNKNOWN GOD" (Acts 17:23).
- **Raise the need**: why does this matter to my hearers?
- **Introduce the passage** and main idea.

Avoid long apologies ("I'm not prepared...") or jokes unrelated to the message.

**Conclusions** (2 to 4 minutes):

- **Restate** the main idea clearly.
- **Apply** specifically: what should people believe, do or change this week?
- **Call for response** where appropriate: repentance, faith, commitment, prayer.
- **End strongly**; don't keep saying "finally".

Joshua concluded his farewell with a call: "choose you this day whom ye will serve... but as for me and my house, we will serve the LORD" (Joshua 24:15).

Respect your **time**. A focused 25-minute message usually helps more than a scattered 60-minute one.

# Delivery | Speaking with clarity and confidence.
## Voice and body | Nehemiah 8:3-5; Acts 26:1
When Ezra read the law, he stood "upon a pulpit of wood... in the sight of all the people" and "the ears of all the people were attentive" (Nehemiah 8:3-5). Paul "stretched forth the hand, and answered for himself" (Acts 26:1). Delivery matters.

**Voice**:

- **Volume**: be heard at the back, with or without a microphone.
- **Pace**: not too fast; pause after important statements.
- **Variety**: change pitch and pace to keep attention and show emotion.
- **Clarity**: pronounce clearly; avoid filler words ("eh", "you know", "praise the Lord" used as filler).

**Body**:

- **Stand naturally**, without leaning or pacing constantly.
- **Eye contact**: look at different parts of the audience.
- **Gestures** that fit your words.
- **Facial expression** that matches the message.

Use **notes** wisely: an outline you glance at, not a script you read with your head down.
## Nerves, practice and feedback | 2 Timothy 1:7; Proverbs 27:17
"God hath not given us the spirit of fear; but of power, and of love, and of a sound mind" (2 Timothy 1:7). Almost every speaker feels nervous. Nerves show you care.

Handling nerves:

- **Prepare thoroughly**; confidence grows from preparation.
- **Practise aloud** several times, ideally standing up.
- **Arrive early**; check the microphone and the room.
- **Pray**, breathe slowly, and remember you are serving, not performing.
- **Focus on the message and the people**, not on yourself.

**Get feedback**:

- Record yourself and watch it.
- Ask a trusted listener: Was the main idea clear? What helped? What distracted?
- Learn from others: watch skilled speakers.

"Iron sharpeneth iron" (Proverbs 27:17). Every message is practice for the next.

# Speaking in the field | Outreaches, crusades, classrooms and special settings.
## Adapting to your audience | 1 Corinthians 9:19-23; Acts 14:15-17; Acts 17:22-23
"I am made all things to all men, that I might by all means save some" (1 Corinthians 9:22). Paul spoke differently in a synagogue (Acts 13), to farmers in Lystra (Acts 14:15-17, about rain and harvests) and to philosophers in Athens (Acts 17). The gospel stayed the same; the approach changed.

Ask:

- **Who** are they? Age, education, faith background, language.
- **What do they already know** about the Bible?
- **What concerns them**? Exams, jobs, family, health, fear.
- **How long** will they listen? Children and outdoor crowds need shorter, livelier messages.

Speak in the **language people understand** best, use an interpreter when needed, and choose illustrations from their world.
## Outreach, crusade and the appeal | Acts 2:37-41; Acts 8:5-12; Matthew 3:1-2
At an outreach or crusade, the message is usually **evangelistic**: aimed at people who don't yet follow Christ.

A simple evangelistic message:

- Start with a **need** people feel (fear, guilt, emptiness, purpose).
- Present **who Jesus is** and **what He did** (His death and resurrection).
- Explain **the response**: repent and believe (Acts 2:38; Mark 1:15).
- Give a **clear, honest invitation**.

The **appeal**:

- Explain clearly what you are asking people to do and why.
- Do not manipulate with pressure, long emotional repetition, or false promises.
- Make sure **counsellors** are ready to meet those who respond.
- Pray with them, and ensure follow-up.

Working with an **interpreter**: use short sentences, pause for them, avoid idioms, and meet beforehand to go through key terms.

"They that gladly received his word were baptized" (Acts 2:41): the goal is genuine faith and discipleship, not just a show of hands.
