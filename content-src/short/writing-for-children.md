# Knowing your readers | Children, ages and how they read.
## Writing for little ones | Deuteronomy 6:6-7; Matthew 19:13-15; 2 Timothy 3:15
"These words... thou shalt teach them diligently unto thy children, and shalt talk of them when thou sittest in thine house, and when thou walkest by the way" (Deuteronomy 6:6-7). Jesus said, "Suffer little children, and forbid them not, to come unto me" (Matthew 19:14). Timothy knew the Scriptures "from a child" (2 Timothy 3:15).

What children read shapes how they see God and the world. Writing for children is not "easy writing"; it is concentrated writing.

Age groups (approximate):

- **Board books (0 to 3)**: very few words, naming, rhythm, repetition.
- **Picture books (3 to 7)**: usually 300 to 800 words read aloud by an adult; pictures tell half the story.
- **Early readers (5 to 8)**: short sentences, familiar words, for children reading alone.
- **Chapter books (7 to 10)**: short chapters, simple plots, some illustrations.
- **Middle grade (8 to 12)**: longer novels, more complex characters and themes.
## What children love | Mark 10:16; Proverbs 22:6; Psalm 78:4
Children love:

- **Characters they can relate to**: often a child or a child-like animal facing a problem.
- **Action and movement**: something happens on every page.
- **Repetition and pattern**: "The big billy goat..." patterns let children join in.
- **Rhythm and sound**: words that are fun to say aloud.
- **Humour**: silliness, surprise, funny names.
- **Safety and warmth** at the end.
- **Respect**: they hate being talked down to.

They dislike long descriptions, moralising, and adults solving every problem. Let the **child character** solve the problem where possible.

"We will not hide them from their children, shewing to the generation to come the praises of the LORD" (Psalm 78:4). Delight and truth go together.

# Bible stories for children | Retelling Scripture faithfully.
## Faithful retelling | Proverbs 30:5-6; Revelation 22:18-19; 2 Timothy 2:15
"Every word of God is pure... Add thou not unto his words, lest he reprove thee, and thou be found a liar" (Proverbs 30:5-6).

When retelling Bible stories:

- **Read the passage carefully** in a reliable translation, several times.
- **Keep the main point** of the passage. The story of David and Goliath is mainly about God's power and honour (1 Samuel 17:45-47), not only "be brave".
- **Simplify without distorting**: you can leave out details, but don't change what happened.
- **Avoid adding details as fact**: you may imagine feelings or small actions, but don't add events, names or dialogue that change the story. A note can say "Imagine how..." when you speculate.
- **Point to God**, not just to the hero. Bible characters were flawed; God is the true hero.
- **Point to Jesus** where the whole Bible story leads there.
- **Be age-appropriate** with violent or difficult passages: tell the truth gently.
## Making Bible stories memorable | Luke 15:3-7; Psalm 145:4
Jesus' parable of the lost sheep is perfect for children: one sheep, a searching shepherd, a happy ending (Luke 15:3-7).

Techniques:

- **Start with action**: "One sheep wandered away..."
- **Use short sentences** and vivid verbs.
- **Use dialogue** from the Bible, simplified carefully.
- **Repeat key phrases**: "He searched and searched and searched."
- **Ask questions** in the text or afterwards: "How do you think the shepherd felt?"
- **End with one clear truth**: "God loves you and comes looking for you."
- **Add a short Bible verse** at the end, in a clear translation.

"One generation shall praise thy works to another" (Psalm 145:4). A well-told Bible story can stay with a child for life.

# Picture books and publishing | Writing the text and working with illustrators.
## Writing a picture book | Habakkuk 2:2; Proverbs 15:23
Picture book texts are short, so every word counts.

Craft tips:

- **One clear story**: a character, a problem, attempts, a solution, a warm ending.
- **Read-aloud quality**: rhythm, repetition, fun sounds. Read it aloud many times.
- **Leave room for pictures**: don't describe what the picture will show ("She wore a red dress"). Let the illustrator show it.
- **Page turns**: a typical picture book has 32 pages, with about 12 to 14 spreads of story. Plan where each page turns; make readers want to turn the page ("And then... she heard a noise!").
- **Rhyme carefully**: bad rhyme is worse than none. If you rhyme, keep the metre steady and never twist meaning.
- **Local settings and names**: Nigerian children deserve stories with names, foods, places and families like theirs.
- **Cut ruthlessly**: aim under 600 words.
## Illustration and publishing | Exodus 35:30-35; Proverbs 22:29
God gave Bezaleel and Aholiab skill "to work all manner of work, of the engraver, and of the cunning workman, and of the embroiderer" (Exodus 35:35). Illustrators are partners.

Preparing your manuscript:

- **Dummy book**: fold sheets into a booklet and write the text on each page to test page turns.
- **Illustration notes**: only where the picture must show something the text doesn't say. Keep them brief, in brackets.
- **Format**: double-spaced, page numbers, your contact details.

Publishing routes:

- **Traditional publishers**: submit to children's or Christian publishers (follow their guidelines). Usually they choose the illustrator.
- **Self-publishing**: you hire and pay an illustrator; agree in writing on price, rights and credit.
- **Churches and schools**: Sunday school curricula, school readers, ministry booklets.

**Test with children**: read your story to children of the right age and watch their faces. They are honest critics.
