# The art of persuasion | Classical rhetoric and the Christian.
## The three appeals | Acts 17:22-31; Acts 26:1-29
Aristotle described three **appeals**:

- **Ethos** (character): why should readers trust you? Credibility, honesty, goodwill. Paul before Agrippa began, "I think myself happy, king Agrippa, because I shall answer for myself this day before thee" (Acts 26:2), courteous and personal.
- **Pathos** (emotion): connecting with readers' feelings, values and hopes. Paul spoke of his own story and longing: "I would to God, that not only thou, but also all that hear me this day, were... altogether such as I am, except these bonds" (Acts 26:29).
- **Logos** (reason): logic and evidence. In Athens, Paul reasoned from their altar and their poets to the Creator and the resurrection (Acts 17:22-31).

Strong persuasion uses all three. Logic without warmth feels cold; emotion without logic feels manipulative; both without credibility won't be trusted.
## Persuasion with integrity | 2 Corinthians 4:2; 1 Peter 3:15-16; Colossians 4:6
Paul refused "craftiness" and "handling the word of God deceitfully", commending himself "to every man's conscience in the sight of God" (2 Corinthians 4:2). Peter calls us to give a reason for our hope "with meekness and fear; Having a good conscience" (1 Peter 3:15-16).

Christian persuasion is different from manipulation:

- **Truthful**: no invented statistics, quotes or stories.
- **Fair**: represent opposing views accurately, even generously.
- **Respectful**: attack arguments, not people.
- **Free**: invite, don't coerce. Leave room for the reader's conscience.
- **Humble**: admit what you don't know.

The goal is not just to win but to help people see what is true and good. "Let your speech be alway with grace, seasoned with salt, that ye may know how ye ought to answer every man" (Colossians 4:6).

# Building arguments | Claims, reasons, evidence and objections.
## Claim, reasons, evidence | Acts 17:2-3; Isaiah 1:18
In Thessalonica Paul "reasoned with them out of the scriptures, Opening and alleging, that Christ must needs have suffered, and risen again" (Acts 17:2-3). God Himself invites, "Come now, and let us reason together" (Isaiah 1:18).

An argument has:

- **Claim (thesis)**: what you want readers to believe or do. Make it specific: not "Education is important" but "Every local church should run a free after-school tutoring programme for children in its community."
- **Reasons**: why the claim is true (usually two to four).
- **Evidence**: facts, statistics, examples, expert testimony, Scripture, experience, each supporting a reason. Cite reliable sources.
- **Warrant**: the link between evidence and claim (sometimes unstated; check it's sound).

Test each reason: if a reader doubts it, what evidence will convince them?
## Answering objections | Proverbs 18:17; Romans 6:1-2; Romans 3:5-8
"He that is first in his own cause seemeth just; but his neighbour cometh and searcheth him" (Proverbs 18:17). A persuasive writer anticipates the neighbour's questions.

Paul did this constantly in Romans: "What shall we say then? Shall we continue in sin, that grace may abound? God forbid" (Romans 6:1-2). He raised the strongest objection and answered it.

Handling objections (**rebuttal**):

1. **State the objection fairly**, in its strongest form ("Some argue that churches lack money for tutoring...").
2. **Concede** what is true in it ("It's true that budgets are tight...").
3. **Respond**: show why your claim still holds ("...but volunteer tutors and donated space make the cost small").

Answering objections builds ethos: readers see you are fair and have thought carefully. Ignoring obvious objections makes your argument look weak.

# Fallacies and writing the piece | Avoiding bad arguments and writing well.
## Common fallacies | Proverbs 14:15; 1 Thessalonians 5:21
"Prove all things; hold fast that which is good" (1 Thessalonians 5:21). A **fallacy** is a flaw in reasoning. Avoid them in your writing and spot them in others':

- **Ad hominem**: attacking the person instead of the argument.
- **Straw man**: distorting an opponent's view to make it easy to attack.
- **False dilemma**: presenting only two options when more exist.
- **Slippery slope**: claiming one step will inevitably lead to disaster, without evidence.
- **Hasty generalisation**: drawing a broad conclusion from too few examples.
- **Appeal to popularity**: "Everyone believes it, so it's true."
- **Appeal to authority** (misused): citing someone who isn't an expert on the topic.
- **Circular reasoning**: the conclusion is assumed in the premise.
- **Red herring**: distracting with an irrelevant point.
- **Post hoc**: assuming that because B followed A, A caused B.

"The simple believeth every word: but the prudent man looketh well to his going" (Proverbs 14:15).
## Writing the persuasive piece | Acts 26:24-29; Joshua 24:14-15
Structure for a persuasive essay or opinion article (800 to 1,200 words):

1. **Introduction**: a hook (story, fact, question), context, and your clear **thesis**.
2. **Reasons**: one paragraph or section per reason, each with evidence and explanation.
3. **Rebuttal**: the strongest objection, fairly stated and answered.
4. **Conclusion**: restate the thesis in fresh words, show what's at stake, and give a **call to action**: what should readers believe or do?

Joshua's farewell is classic persuasion: history (evidence), the stakes, a choice, and his own example: "as for me and my house, we will serve the LORD" (Joshua 24:15).

For **opinion pieces** in newspapers or blogs: one clear argument, a timely hook, short paragraphs, and your credentials in one line. Check the publication's guidelines.

Revise for fairness as well as force: would your opponent say you represented their view honestly?

@assignment Practical assignment: a persuasive essay
Write a persuasive essay or opinion article of 800 to 1,200 words on an issue that matters to your church, campus or community. It must include a clear thesis, at least three reasons with cited evidence, a fair rebuttal of the strongest objection, and a call to action.

Then add a 200 to 300 word analysis of your own piece: where you used ethos, pathos and logos, and how you made sure it was fair and free of fallacies.
