# Notes and rhythm | The building blocks of written music.
## The staff and note names | Psalm 33:2-3
Music is written on a **staff** (or stave) of five lines and four spaces. A **clef** at the start tells you which notes the lines represent:

- **Treble clef** (G clef): for higher voices and instruments (soprano, alto, right hand on keyboard).
- **Bass clef** (F clef): for lower voices and instruments (tenor, bass, left hand).

Notes are named with the letters **A B C D E F G**, then repeat.

Treble clef:

- Lines from bottom: **E G B D F** ("Every Good Boy Deserves Favour").
- Spaces from bottom: **F A C E** ("FACE").

Bass clef:

- Lines: **G B D F A** ("Good Boys Do Fine Always").
- Spaces: **A C E G** ("All Cows Eat Grass").

**Middle C** sits on a short extra line (a ledger line) between the two staves. "Play skilfully" (Psalm 33:3): reading music is a skill anyone can learn with practice.
## Rhythm and time signatures | Ecclesiastes 3:1-4
"To every thing there is a season, and a time" (Ecclesiastes 3:1). In music, **rhythm** is the pattern of long and short sounds.

Common note values (in 4/4 time):

- **Semibreve** (whole note): 4 beats
- **Minim** (half note): 2 beats
- **Crotchet** (quarter note): 1 beat
- **Quaver** (eighth note): ½ beat
- **Semiquaver** (sixteenth note): ¼ beat

Each has a matching **rest** (silence) of the same length. A **dot** after a note adds half its value (a dotted minim = 3 beats).

The **time signature** at the start shows the beats in each bar:

- **4/4**: four crotchet beats per bar (most common).
- **3/4**: three beats (waltz feel, many hymns).
- **6/8**: two groups of three quavers (a lilting feel).

Clap and count aloud: "1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and".

# Sol-fa and staff | Connecting tonic sol-fa with written notes.
## Tonic sol-fa | Psalm 150:3-6
**Tonic sol-fa** names notes by their position in the scale, not by fixed letters:

**d r m f s l t d'** (*doh ray me fah soh lah te doh*)

The first note (*doh*) can be any pitch; it is the "home" note of the key. This makes sol-fa excellent for singing in any key and for hearing relationships between notes. Many Nigerian choirs and church music traditions teach parts in sol-fa.

Notation:

- Higher octave: **d'** (a mark above or after)
- Lower octave: **d,** (a mark below or after)
- Rhythm in sol-fa uses **colons and dots** between notes: `| d : r | m : - |` where **:** separates beats and **-** continues a note.

Practise: sing *d m s d'*, then *d' s m d*. Then *d r m f s* slowly with a keyboard.
## From sol-fa to staff | 1 Corinthians 14:7-8
"Except they give a distinction in the sounds, how shall it be known what is piped or harped?" (1 Corinthians 14:7). Knowing both systems makes you a stronger musician.

To connect them:

1. Find the **key** (Module 3 explains key signatures).
2. The key note on the staff is **doh**.
3. Count up the scale: if C is *doh*, then D is *ray*, E is *me*, F is *fah*, G is *soh*, A is *lah*, B is *te*.
4. In G major, G is *doh*, A is *ray*, B is *me*, C is *fah*, D is *soh*, E is *lah*, F♯ is *te*.

Practice:

- Take a hymn printed in staff notation. Write the sol-fa under the melody.
- Take a sol-fa part and write it on the staff in C major.

Over time you will "hear" the notes as you read either system.

# Scales and keys | Major scales and key signatures.
## The major scale | Psalm 98:4-5
A **major scale** has eight notes from one note to the same note an octave higher, following a pattern of **tones** (T, whole steps) and **semitones** (S, half steps):

**T T S T T T S**

On a keyboard, a semitone is the nearest key (white or black); a tone is two semitones.

C major uses only white keys: **C D E F G A B C**. The semitones fall between **E–F** and **B–C**, matching *me–fah* and *te–doh'* in sol-fa.

Starting on G, the pattern needs **F♯** (F sharp) to keep the semitone between the 7th and 8th notes: **G A B C D E F♯ G**.

Starting on F, it needs **B♭** (B flat): **F G A B♭ C D E F**.

**Sharp (♯)** raises a note a semitone; **flat (♭)** lowers it a semitone; **natural (♮)** cancels them.
## Key signatures | 1 Chronicles 15:16
Instead of writing a sharp or flat every time, music uses a **key signature** at the start of each line.

Common major keys:

- **C**: no sharps or flats
- **G**: 1 sharp (F♯)
- **D**: 2 sharps (F♯ C♯)
- **A**: 3 sharps (F♯ C♯ G♯)
- **F**: 1 flat (B♭)
- **B♭**: 2 flats (B♭ E♭)
- **E♭**: 3 flats (B♭ E♭ A♭)

Quick ways to find *doh*:

- **Sharps**: the last sharp is *te*; *doh* is one semitone above.
- **Flats**: the second-last flat is *doh* (for F major, remember it has one flat).

The Levites appointed singers "with instruments of musick, psalteries and harps and cymbals" (1 Chronicles 15:16). Instruments and singers must agree on the key. Choosing a good key for the congregation is one of a music leader's most practical skills.

# Chords | Building chords and understanding harmony.
## Triads | Ecclesiastes 4:12
"A threefold cord is not quickly broken" (Ecclesiastes 4:12). The most basic chord is a **triad**: three notes stacked in thirds.

Build a major triad on any note: **root + 3rd + 5th** of its major scale.

- C major chord: **C E G** (*d m s*)
- F major chord: **F A C** (*f l d'*)
- G major chord: **G B D** (*s t r'*)

In every major key, three chords do much of the work:

- **I** (built on *doh*)
- **IV** (built on *fah*)
- **V** (built on *soh*)

In C major these are C, F and G. In G major: G, C and D. Many hymns and choruses can be accompanied with I, IV and V alone.

The chords on *ray*, *me* and *lah* are **minor** (ii, iii, vi). In C major: Dm, Em, Am. Minor chords sound softer or sadder; they add colour.
## Chord charts and practice | Psalm 92:1-3; 2 Timothy 2:15
Worship bands often use **chord charts**: lyrics with chord names written above the words where the chord changes.

```
C              F        C
Praise the Lord, all ye nations
        Am       F      G     C
Praise Him, all ye people, Amen
```

Sometimes charts use **numbers** (the Nashville system: 1, 4, 5, 6m) so a song can be played in any key.

To read and play:

- Know the **key** and its I, IV, V and vi chords.
- Watch **where** each chord changes on the lyric.
- Keep a steady **rhythm**.

A practice routine:

1. Play I–IV–V–I slowly in C, G and F.
2. Add vi (Am in C) and ii (Dm in C).
3. Take a simple hymn, find its key and chords, and play along.
4. Write a chord chart for a song your church sings.

"It is a good thing to give thanks unto the LORD... upon an instrument of ten strings, and upon the psaltery; upon the harp with a solemn sound" (Psalm 92:1-3).
