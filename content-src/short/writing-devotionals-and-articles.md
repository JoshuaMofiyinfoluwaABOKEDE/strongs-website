# One big idea | The shape of a devotional and the power of one point.
## What a devotional is | Psalm 119:130; Hebrews 4:12
A **devotional** is a short piece (usually 250 to 500 words) that helps readers meet God through one passage of Scripture and apply it to life. "The entrance of thy words giveth light" (Psalm 119:130). The devotional's job is to open the door to the Word, not to replace it.

A common shape:

1. **Scripture**: a short passage with the reference.
2. **Opening**: a story, observation or question that connects with life.
3. **Explanation**: what the passage means in context.
4. **Application**: how it changes the way we think or live today.
5. **Response**: a short prayer, reflection question or action step.

Devotionals are read quickly, often on a phone in the morning. They must be clear, warm and focused. Readers should finish with one truth they can carry all day.
## Choosing your one idea | Luke 10:41-42; Psalm 46:10
Martha was "careful and troubled about many things: But one thing is needful" (Luke 10:41-42). Many devotionals fail because they try to say everything about a passage.

To find your one idea:

- Read the passage several times and in context.
- Ask: what is the **central truth** here about God, about us, or about how to live?
- Write it as **one sentence**: "Stillness before God is an act of trust" (Psalm 46:10).
- Check that the passage really teaches it, not just that a word appears in it.

Then let every paragraph serve that one sentence. Save your other good ideas for future devotionals. A devotional with one clear point is remembered; one with five points is forgotten by lunchtime.

An **article** is longer (800 to 1,500 words) and may develop several points, but it still needs one controlling idea.

# Handling the text | Using Scripture faithfully in short writing.
## Context before comment | 2 Timothy 2:15; Philippians 4:13
Short pieces tempt us to pull verses out of context. "I can do all things through Christ which strengtheneth me" (Philippians 4:13) is often used as a promise of success in sport or exams. In context (verses 11-12), Paul is speaking of contentment in hunger and plenty, poverty and abundance. Its real meaning is even richer: Christ strengthens us to be content in any circumstance.

Before you write:

- **Read the chapter** around your verse.
- **Ask who** was speaking, to whom, and why.
- **Check the kind of writing**: promise, command, poem, proverb, story.
- **Compare a reliable translation** or a study Bible note.
- **Make sure your application** follows from the meaning.

"Rightly dividing the word of truth" (2 Timothy 2:15) is not only for preachers. Writers reach many readers, so we carry responsibility.
## Illustrations and application | Matthew 13:3-9; James 1:22-25
Jesus taught with stories from ordinary life: a sower, seed, soil, birds. Good **illustrations** make truth visible.

Sources of illustrations:

- Your own life (be honest and humble).
- Nature, work, family, school, the market, the farm.
- History and biography (check your facts).
- Other people's stories (only with permission, and change details if needed for privacy).

Avoid illustrations that overpower the Scripture or become the main point.

**Application** should be specific. Not "Let us trust God more" but "Before you check your phone tomorrow morning, read Psalm 46 and pray about one worry." James warns against being a forgetful hearer (James 1:22-25). Give readers something they can do, think or pray today.

# Titles, openings and endings | Drawing readers in and sending them out.
## Titles and openings | Proverbs 25:25; John 4:7-10
"As cold waters to a thirsty soul, so is good news from a far country" (Proverbs 25:25). Readers decide in seconds whether to keep reading.

**Titles** should be short, clear and intriguing:

- A promise: "Peace When Plans Fall Apart"
- A question: "Why Does God Make Us Wait?"
- A phrase from the text: "One Thing Is Needful"

Avoid titles that promise more than you deliver or that mislead.

**Openings** hook the reader in the first two sentences:

- A short story: "The bus broke down twenty kilometres from Ibadan."
- A question: "When did you last sit still for five minutes?"
- A surprising fact or observation.
- A line of dialogue.

Jesus opened with "Give me to drink" (John 4:7), a simple request that led to living water. Start with what the reader recognises, then lead them deeper.
## Endings that land | Joshua 24:15; Ecclesiastes 12:13
Endings are what readers remember. Ecclesiastes ends: "Let us hear the conclusion of the whole matter: Fear God, and keep his commandments" (Ecclesiastes 12:13). Joshua ended with a choice: "choose you this day whom ye will serve" (Joshua 24:15).

Strong endings:

- **Return to the opening** story or image, now seen in a new light.
- **Restate the main idea** in fresh words.
- **Give one clear step**: a prayer, a question, an action.
- **End with Scripture** that seals the point.

Avoid: introducing a new idea at the end, a long summary of everything, or a weak "I hope this helps."

A short closing **prayer** is common in devotionals. Keep it brief and personal, in plain language the reader can pray with you.

# Workshop | Writing, revising and submitting a series.
## Writing a devotional series | Lamentations 3:22-23; Acts 17:11
Many publications want devotionals in **series**: a week on one psalm, a month on a theme. God's mercies "are new every morning" (Lamentations 3:22-23), and readers come back daily.

Planning a seven-day series:

1. Choose a **book or theme** (for example, Psalm 23, or "Prayer in Acts").
2. Pick seven **passages** in order, each with its own one idea.
3. Give each day a **title**, a **main sentence** and a **response**.
4. Keep a **consistent format and length** across all seven.
5. Make sure the series **builds**: readers should sense movement, not repetition.

Write all seven drafts quickly, then revise them together. Check every reference. Encourage readers, like the Bereans, to read the passage themselves (Acts 17:11).
## Submitting your work | Proverbs 16:3; Galatians 6:9
"Commit thy works unto the LORD, and thy thoughts shall be established" (Proverbs 16:3).

Before you submit:

- **Read the guidelines**: word count, Bible version, format, deadlines.
- **Proofread carefully**: names, references, spelling, punctuation.
- **Write a short cover note**: who you are, what you are submitting, and why it suits their readers.
- **Submit only original work** and declare if it has appeared elsewhere.

Where to submit:

- Church and fellowship devotional booklets.
- Ministry and campus magazines.
- Christian blogs and websites.
- STRONGS Writers' community publications.
- Your own blog, email list or WhatsApp channel.

Rejection is normal. Learn from feedback and keep writing. "Let us not be weary in well doing: for in due season we shall reap, if we faint not" (Galatians 6:9).

@assignment Practical assignment: a five-day devotional series
Write a five-day devotional series on one book or theme. Each devotional should be 300 to 450 words and include: a title, a Scripture reference, an opening hook, explanation in context, a specific application, and a short prayer or reflection question.

Add a 150-word cover note explaining your theme, your intended readers, and where you would submit the series.
