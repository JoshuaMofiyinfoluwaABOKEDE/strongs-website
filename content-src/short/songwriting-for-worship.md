# Songs that teach | Why worship songs must be true.
## The songwriter's responsibility | Colossians 3:16; Exodus 15:1-2; Revelation 15:3
After crossing the Red Sea, "sang Moses and the children of Israel this song unto the LORD" (Exodus 15:1). In Revelation the redeemed sing "the song of Moses... and the song of the Lamb" (Revelation 15:3). God's people have always turned His acts into songs.

Songs **teach** (Colossians 3:16). A congregation may sing your words hundreds of times. That is a great privilege and responsibility. A songwriter is a kind of teacher.

Before writing, ask:

- **What is true about God here?** His character, His works, His promises.
- **What passage** of Scripture is this song rooted in?
- **Who will sing it?** A congregation, a choir, a soloist, children?
- **What response** should it invite: praise, confession, trust, commitment?

Great worship songs often grow from long meditation on one passage or one truth.
## Theology in a few lines | Psalm 145:1-7; Philippians 2:5-11
Some scholars think Philippians 2:6-11 may have been an early Christian hymn. Whether or not it was sung, it shows how much truth a few lines can carry: Christ's divinity, humility, death, exaltation and lordship.

Keep your song **true**:

- **Say what Scripture says**, not just what feels good. Avoid promising what God has not promised.
- **Keep God at the centre.** It is fine to sing about our response, but the focus should be on who He is and what He has done.
- **Balance**: celebration and lament, joy and repentance, personal and corporate ("I" and "we").
- **Be specific**: "You are good" is true; "You fed five thousand with five loaves" shows *how*.
- **Ask a pastor or theologically trained friend** to review your lyrics.

Psalm 145 says, "One generation shall praise thy works to another, and shall declare thy mighty acts" (verse 4). Songs carry the faith from one generation to the next.

# Lyrics | Crafting words that sing.
## Images, clarity and the hook | Psalm 23:1-6; Psalm 46:1-3
Psalm 23 uses one image (a shepherd) throughout: green pastures, still waters, rod and staff, a table, an anointing, a house. One strong image carries the whole song.

Lyric craft:

- **Choose a central image or idea** and stay with it.
- **Use concrete words**: rock, river, fire, bread, light, storm.
- **Write a strong hook**: a short, memorable line (often the title) that sums up the song and returns in the chorus.
- **Be clear**: congregations sing at first sight on a screen. Avoid obscure words and twisted word order.
- **Avoid clichés** where possible: find a fresh way to say an old truth.
- **Make lines singable**: open vowels ("ah", "oh") on long, high notes; avoid clusters of consonants there.
## Rhyme, rhythm and language | Psalm 119:1-8; Lamentations 1:1
Hebrew poets used patterns: Psalm 119 is an acrostic, each section starting with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet. Structure helps memory.

- **Rhyme** helps people remember, but never force a rhyme that changes the meaning. Near rhymes (*grace* / *place*, *love* / *above*) are fine.
- **Rhythm of words**: stressed syllables should fall on strong beats. Say the line aloud; if the natural stress fights the melody, rewrite.
- **Repetition** is powerful in choruses, but verses should develop the idea.
- **Language**: songs in Yoruba, Igbo, Hausa, Pidgin and other languages can touch people deeply. In tonal languages, fit the melody to the tones so meaning is not changed. Bilingual songs can unite congregations.

Read your lyric aloud as a poem before you set it to music. If it doesn't make sense spoken, it won't make sense sung.

# Melody and form | Writing tunes congregations can sing.
## Singable melodies | Psalm 95:1-2; Psalm 100:1-2
"O come, let us sing unto the LORD: let us make a joyful noise to the rock of our salvation" (Psalm 95:1). Congregational melodies must be singable by ordinary people, not only trained singers.

Guidelines:

- **Range**: keep most of the melody within about an octave plus a note or two; around A below middle C to D above the octave suits most congregations.
- **Steps and small leaps**: mostly stepwise motion, with a few memorable leaps.
- **Clear rhythm**: avoid very complex syncopation for congregational songs.
- **Repetition with variety**: repeat melodic ideas so people learn quickly, but vary them so the song develops.
- **A high point**: often in the chorus or bridge, on an important word.
- **Breathing places**: leave space between phrases.

Test it: can a friend who doesn't read music sing it back after hearing it twice?
## Song form | Psalm 136:1-9; Psalm 107:1-15
Psalm 136 repeats "for his mercy endureth for ever" after every line, a refrain. Psalm 107 repeats a pattern of trouble, crying out, deliverance and thanksgiving. Form helps people follow.

Common forms:

- **Verse–chorus**: verses tell or develop; the chorus repeats the main idea.
- **Verse–chorus–bridge**: a bridge adds a new angle or climax before a final chorus.
- **Hymn (strophic)**: several verses on the same tune, each developing the theme, often ending with a doxology or hope of heaven.
- **Call and response**: a leader sings, the congregation answers. Very common in African worship.

Plan your **lyric's journey**: verse 1 might describe a need, verse 2 what God did, the bridge our response, and the final chorus a declaration of praise. Each section should earn its place.

# Finishing a song | Revision, testing and sharing.
## Revising and testing | Proverbs 15:22; Psalm 40:3
"Without counsel purposes are disappointed: but in the multitude of counsellors they are established" (Proverbs 15:22).

Steps to finish:

1. **Record a rough demo** on your phone: voice and one instrument.
2. **Leave it for a few days**, then listen fresh.
3. **Check the lyrics**: true, clear, specific, singable.
4. **Check the melody**: range, flow, high point, breathing.
5. **Get feedback** from a pastor (theology), a musician (craft) and an ordinary church member (singability).
6. **Revise**. Most good songs go through many drafts.
7. **Test it** in a small group or fellowship before a large service.
8. **Watch the people**: do they join in by the second chorus?

"He hath put a new song in my mouth, even praise unto our God: many shall see it, and fear, and shall trust in the LORD" (Psalm 40:3). Aim for songs that help many trust the Lord.
## Sharing, rights and integrity | Exodus 20:15; Romans 13:7
When you share your songs:

- **Write a lead sheet**: melody, lyrics and chords, with your name, date and the Scripture reference.
- **Keep dated copies** (recordings and written versions). They show the song is yours.
- **Copyright**: in most countries, including Nigeria, an original song is protected by copyright automatically once it is written down or recorded. Registration with the national copyright body can add evidence. Seek advice before signing any publishing agreement.
- **Respect others' rights**: don't copy another writer's melody or lyrics. "Thou shalt not steal" (Exodus 20:15). If you co-write, agree on credit and shares in writing.
- **Churches using songs** may need licences for projecting or recording copyrighted songs; ask your church about this.
- **Give credit** to co-writers and to any hymn or Scripture you adapt.

Give God the glory for every song He gives you. Hold your songs with open hands; they are for His people.

@assignment Practical assignment: write a worship song
Write an original worship song rooted in one passage of Scripture. Hand in:

1. The passage and a one-sentence statement of the song's main truth.
2. The full lyrics, with sections labelled (verse, chorus, bridge).
3. A lead sheet or chord chart with the key, and a phone recording of you (or someone else) singing it.
4. A 250 to 400 word reflection: how you developed the song, feedback you received from at least two people, and the changes you made.
