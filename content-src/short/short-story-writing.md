# Idea and character | Where stories begin.
## Why Christians write fiction | Matthew 13:34-35; Luke 15:1-32; 2 Samuel 12:1-7
"All these things spake Jesus unto the multitude in parables; and without a parable spake he not unto them" (Matthew 13:34). The parables are short fiction: invented characters (a sower, a lost son, a rich fool) who reveal truth about God and people.

Fiction can:

- Help readers **feel** truth they already know in their heads.
- Let readers see **themselves** in a character and change, as David did in Nathan's story (2 Samuel 12).
- Explore **hard questions** honestly: suffering, injustice, temptation, doubt.
- Reach readers who would never open a theology book.

Christian fiction doesn't need to be "safe" or preachy. It needs to be **true**: true to life, true to human nature, and ultimately true to the God who redeems. Flannery O'Connor and others showed that faith can shape fiction deeply without sermonising.
## Characters with desire | Luke 15:11-24; 1 Samuel 18:6-9
A story begins when a **character wants something** and something stands in the way.

The prodigal son wanted freedom and his inheritance. The obstacle was himself: his choices led to famine and pig pens. Saul wanted honour; when women sang that David had slain ten thousands, "Saul was very wroth" (1 Samuel 18:8), and jealousy destroyed him.

Building a character:

- **Desire**: what do they want most in this story?
- **Fear or wound**: what are they afraid of? What hurt them?
- **Contradiction**: real people are mixed (a generous man who lies; a strict mother who secretly laughs at herself).
- **Specifics**: name, age, job, how they talk, what they carry in their bag.
- **Change**: how will the story change them, or fail to?

Short stories usually have **few characters** (two to four) and focus on **one main character**. Know them well, even details that never appear on the page.

# Point of view and plot | Who tells the story, and what happens.
## Point of view | Ruth 1:1-22; Nehemiah 1:1-4
**Point of view (POV)** is whose eyes we see through.

- **First person** ("I"): intimate, limited to what the narrator knows. Nehemiah writes, "I sat down and wept" (Nehemiah 1:4).
- **Third person limited** ("she"): close to one character's thoughts and feelings. The book of Ruth stays close to Naomi and Ruth.
- **Third person omniscient**: the narrator knows everything about everyone. Harder to do well in short stories.
- **Second person** ("you"): rare, experimental.

Choose the POV that gives readers the best access to the story's heart. Then **stay consistent**: don't jump into other characters' heads mid-scene ("head-hopping").

Decide your **tense** (past or present) and keep it.
## Plot and the turning point | Esther 4:13-16; Esther 6:1-11
A short story's plot is usually simple:

1. **Opening**: start close to the trouble. Introduce the character and their desire quickly.
2. **Rising complication**: obstacles, choices and consequences build.
3. **Turning point (crisis)**: the character must choose or face a revelation.
4. **Climax**: the decisive moment.
5. **Resolution**: brief; we see what has changed.

Esther's story has a classic turning point. Mordecai says, "who knoweth whether thou art come to the kingdom for such a time as this?", and she decides: "if I perish, I perish" (Esther 4:14-16). Later the sleepless king reads the records and the plot reverses (Esther 6).

Tips:

- **Begin in the middle** of action (*in medias res*), then fill in background briefly.
- **Cause and effect**: each event should lead to the next.
- **Raise the stakes**: what does the character stand to lose?
- **Earn the ending**: avoid sudden miracles or coincidences that solve everything (*deus ex machina*). In real life God often works through choices and process; let your plot respect that.

# Setting, dialogue and theme | Bringing the story to life and to meaning.
## Setting and description | Genesis 28:10-17; Mark 4:35-41
Setting is more than background. It creates mood and pressure. Jacob, alone at night with a stone for a pillow, dreams of a ladder to heaven (Genesis 28:11-12). The disciples' boat fills with water in "a great storm of wind" while Jesus sleeps "on a pillow" (Mark 4:37-38). Setting raises the stakes.

Description that works:

- **Specific and sensory**: the smell of burning bush in harmattan, the rattle of a keke, a generator coughing to life.
- **Selective**: a few telling details, not a full inventory.
- **Filtered through the character**: a frightened character notices different things from a bored one.
- **Woven into action**: describe while things happen, not in long blocks.

Nigerian and African settings are rich and under-told. Write the world you know with precision and love.
## Dialogue, theme and revision | Proverbs 25:11; Matthew 13:9; Proverbs 27:17
**Dialogue** should reveal character and move the story. Keep tags simple ("she said"), use action beats ("He set down the cup."), and let people talk past each other sometimes, as they do in life.

**Theme** is what the story means: grace, pride, forgiveness, faith in suffering. In good fiction, theme **emerges** from character and events rather than being announced. Jesus often ended parables without explanation: "He that hath ears to hear, let him hear" (Matthew 13:9). Trust readers.

Avoid:

- Characters who exist only to preach.
- Conversions that happen too easily.
- Villains with no humanity.

**Revision checklist**:

- Does the opening grab attention within the first paragraph?
- Is the main character's desire clear?
- Does every scene raise the stakes or change something?
- Is the turning point earned?
- Is POV and tense consistent?
- Have I cut unnecessary words, scenes and adjectives?
- Read aloud. Get feedback from two readers. Revise again.
