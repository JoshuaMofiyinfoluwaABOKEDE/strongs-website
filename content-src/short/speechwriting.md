# Occasion and message | What every speech needs before a word is written.
## Occasion, audience, purpose | Ecclesiastes 3:1-7; Proverbs 15:23
"To every thing there is a season... a time to keep silence, and a time to speak" (Ecclesiastes 3:1, 7). "A word spoken in due season, how good is it!" (Proverbs 15:23).

Before writing, answer the **speech brief**:

- **Occasion**: graduation, wedding, funeral, launch, award, church anniversary, conference opening?
- **Audience**: who will be there? How many? What do they know, feel and expect?
- **Purpose**: to inform, inspire, honour, persuade, welcome, thank, comfort?
- **Time**: how long exactly? (About 130 to 150 spoken words per minute; a five-minute speech is roughly 650 to 750 words.)
- **Setting**: indoors or outdoors, microphone, protocol, who speaks before and after?
- **Speaker**: you or someone else? Their style, position and relationship to the audience.

A beautiful speech at the wrong length or wrong tone fails. A simple speech that fits the moment succeeds.
## One central message | Acts 2:36; Joshua 24:14-15
Peter's Pentecost speech built to one sentence: "God hath made that same Jesus, whom ye have crucified, both Lord and Christ" (Acts 2:36). Joshua's farewell built to one choice: "choose you this day whom ye will serve" (Joshua 24:15).

Write your **central message** in one sentence: "Graduates, your degree is a tool; your character will decide what you build with it."

Then choose **two or three supporting points**, each with a story, example or fact.

Listeners can't re-read, so:

- **Say what you'll say, say it, then say what you said** (in fresh words).
- **Signpost**: "Let me share three things...", "The second thing is..."
- **Fewer ideas, more depth**: one memorable message beats ten forgotten ones.

# Structure and style | Writing for the ear.
## Openings and closings | Acts 17:22-23; Ecclesiastes 12:13-14
**Openings** (first 30 to 60 seconds) must win attention:

- A short **story** or personal moment.
- A **question** to the audience.
- A striking **fact** or image.
- A **quotation** (brief, credited) or a Bible verse.
- A gracious **acknowledgement** of the occasion.

Paul in Athens opened by noticing their altar (Acts 17:23): he started where his audience was.

**Protocol**: in many Nigerian settings, speeches begin by recognising dignitaries. Keep it brief and correct ("The Vice-Chancellor, distinguished guests, graduands, ladies and gentlemen"), then move quickly to your opening.

**Closings** are remembered most:

- Return to the opening story or image.
- Restate the central message memorably.
- End with a call, a blessing, a toast or a short powerful line.

Ecclesiastes ends: "Let us hear the conclusion of the whole matter: Fear God, and keep his commandments" (12:13). Know your last line before you start writing.
## Writing for the ear | Psalm 136:1-9; Isaiah 40:1-9; Matthew 5:3-11
Speeches are heard once, so write for the ear:

- **Short sentences** and simple words.
- **Concrete images**, not abstractions.
- **Rhythm**: vary sentence length; read aloud as you write.
- **Personal words**: "you", "we", "us".
- **Rhetorical devices**:
  - **Repetition (anaphora)**: repeating the opening of phrases. The Beatitudes repeat "Blessed are..." (Matthew 5:3-11).
  - **Refrain**: Psalm 136 repeats "for his mercy endureth for ever".
  - **Triads (rule of three)**: "faith, hope, charity" (1 Corinthians 13:13).
  - **Contrast (antithesis)**: "not as a servant, but... a brother beloved" (Philemon 1:16).
  - **Rhetorical questions**: "Who shall separate us from the love of Christ?" (Romans 8:35).
  - **Vivid imagery**: "Every valley shall be exalted, and every mountain and hill shall be made low" (Isaiah 40:4).

Mark pauses in the script with a slash (/) and underline words to stress.

# Occasions and other voices | Special speeches and ghostwriting.
## Occasion speeches | Romans 12:15; 2 Samuel 1:17-27; John 2:1-11
"Rejoice with them that do rejoice, and weep with them that weep" (Romans 12:15). Different occasions need different tones.

- **Welcome address**: greet, honour guests, state the purpose of the gathering, set the tone (3 to 5 minutes).
- **Vote of thanks**: thank specific people and groups accurately and warmly; be brief (2 to 3 minutes). Check names and titles.
- **Graduation or valedictory speech**: memories, gratitude, a central challenge for the future. Avoid inside jokes that exclude.
- **Wedding toast**: a short story about the couple, warmth, a blessing or toast. Never embarrass the couple.
- **Eulogy or tribute**: honour the person's life with specific stories and qualities, comfort the grieving, and point to Christian hope where appropriate. David's lament for Saul and Jonathan (2 Samuel 1:17-27) honours even a flawed king.
- **Award acceptance**: thank, give God glory, share the credit; keep it short.

Respect time limits. Overrunning is the most common complaint about speeches.
## Writing for someone else | 2 Samuel 12:1-7; Proverbs 25:11; Romans 16:22
Tertius wrote down Paul's letter to the Romans and greeted the readers himself: "I Tertius, who wrote this epistle, salute you in the Lord" (Romans 16:22). Writing for others is an old and honourable service.

When drafting for a leader (pastor, principal, executive):

- **Interview them**: what do they want to say? What stories do they tell? What phrases do they use?
- **Listen to their voice**: recordings of past speeches. Match their vocabulary and rhythm.
- **Respect their convictions**: never put words in their mouth they wouldn't believe or say.
- **Check every fact**, name and title. They'll be blamed for errors.
- **Format for delivery**: large font (14 to 16 point), double-spaced, short paragraphs, page numbers, pauses marked, names spelled phonetically if needed.
- **Rehearse** with them if possible; adjust what doesn't sound natural.
- **Confidentiality**: the speech is theirs. Don't claim credit unless agreed.
