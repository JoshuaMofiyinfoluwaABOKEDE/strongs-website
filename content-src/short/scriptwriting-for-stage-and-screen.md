# Story and character | The foundations of every script.
## Premise and logline | 2 Samuel 12:1-7; Luke 10:25-37
When a lawyer asked, "Who is my neighbour?", Jesus told a story: a man attacked on the road, a priest and a Levite who passed by, and a Samaritan who stopped (Luke 10:30-37). The story's **premise** was simple and strong: a despised outsider shows true love.

Every script needs a premise you can state in a **logline**: one sentence naming the main character, their goal, and the obstacle.

- "A proud university student who mocks his Christian roommate must depend on him when a flood traps them in their hostel."
- "A market woman who has cheated customers for years must choose between her business and her new faith when her daughter starts selling with her."

A good logline suggests **conflict** and **change**. If you can't write one, the story isn't clear yet.

Nathan's parable to David (2 Samuel 12) shows the power of a well-chosen premise: it made the king judge himself.
## Characters who want something | Genesis 32:24-30; Luke 19:1-10
Drama comes from characters who **want** something and face **obstacles**. Jacob wrestled all night and would not let go: "I will not let thee go, except thou bless me" (Genesis 32:26). Zacchaeus wanted to see Jesus but "could not for the press, because he was little of stature", so he ran ahead and climbed a tree (Luke 19:3-4).

For each main character, write:

- **Want**: what they consciously pursue (money, love, respect, escape).
- **Need**: what they truly need (forgiveness, humility, God). Often the story moves them from want to need.
- **Flaw**: what stands in their way from inside.
- **Backstory**: what shaped them.
- **Voice**: how they speak (vocabulary, language, rhythm).

Avoid **cardboard characters**: perfect Christians and pure villains. Real people are mixed. Audiences believe characters who struggle, and they are moved when grace meets them.

# Structure and scenes | Shaping the story.
## Three acts | Genesis 37:1-36; Genesis 45:1-8
Most stories follow a **three-act structure**:

- **Act One (setup, about a quarter)**: introduce the world and characters; an **inciting incident** disrupts normal life; the character commits to a goal.
- **Act Two (confrontation, about half)**: obstacles grow; the stakes rise; a **midpoint** twist changes things; often everything seems lost near the end.
- **Act Three (resolution, about a quarter)**: the **climax**, where the main conflict is faced, and the **resolution**, showing the new normal.

Joseph's story fits: dreams and a jealous family (setup), betrayal and slavery (inciting incident), rise and fall in Egypt (confrontation), the brothers before him (climax), and "God did send me before you to preserve life" (resolution, Genesis 45:5).

Outline your story on cards or in a list: one line per scene. Move scenes around until the shape works before you write dialogue.
## Building a scene | Ruth 3:1-18; John 21:15-17
A **scene** is a unit of action in one place and time. Every scene should **change something**: a decision, a revelation, a shift in a relationship.

Ask of each scene:

- **Whose scene is it?** Who wants what?
- **What's the conflict?** Something must resist.
- **What changes** by the end?
- **Where does it start and end?** Enter late, leave early: skip greetings and goodbyes unless they matter.

Jesus' conversation with Peter by the lake (John 21:15-17) is a powerful scene: three questions, Peter's grief, and a commission. A past failure (three denials) is healed by three affirmations, without a single wasted line.

Show, don't tell. Instead of a character saying "I am angry", show him slamming the gate or refusing to eat. Let actions and silence speak.

# Dialogue, format and revision | Writing pages that can be produced.
## Dialogue that lives | Proverbs 15:23; Colossians 4:6; Matthew 5:37
"A word spoken in due season, how good is it!" (Proverbs 15:23).

Good dialogue:

- **Sounds natural but is shaped**: real speech has many "ehn" and "you know"; script dialogue keeps the flavour but cuts the waste.
- **Reveals character**: a lecturer, a bus conductor and a grandmother speak differently.
- **Carries subtext**: people often don't say exactly what they mean. "Have you eaten?" may mean "I'm sorry."
- **Uses language** truthfully: Pidgin, Yoruba, Igbo, Hausa and English mix in real Nigerian speech. Use subtitles or context for screen.
- **Avoids sermons**: let the story carry the message. A short, true line at the right moment does more than a long speech.
- **Is short**: long speeches are rare and must earn their place.

Read dialogue aloud with others. If actors stumble, rewrite.
## Formats and revision | Habakkuk 2:2; Proverbs 15:22
**Stage format** (common style):

```
ACT ONE, SCENE 1
A hostel room. Night. Rain on the roof.
TUNDE: (not looking up) You're praying again?
DAYO: Every night. You could join.
```

**Screenplay format** (film/TV):

```
INT. HOSTEL ROOM - NIGHT
Rain drums on the roof. TUNDE (20) scrolls on his phone. DAYO (21) kneels by the bed.
                    TUNDE
          You're praying again?
```

Screenplays use scene headings (INT./EXT., place, time), action lines in present tense, and centred character names and dialogue. Free tools like **Celtx**, **WriterDuet** or **Trelby** format automatically. Roughly, one formatted page equals one minute of screen time.

**Revision**:

- Hold a **table read** with friends.
- Cut scenes that don't change anything.
- Check that the main character changes.
- Get feedback from a director or experienced writer.

Register and date your script, and agree in writing on credits and rights with any producer.
